Chapter 01 — The Foundation

The Prompt Anatomy Framework

Every prompt that actually works has the same five parts. Most people only ever use one.

🎯
Personalised note loading...

"Write me a caption" gets a generic caption, because that's all the AI has to work with. The model isn't being lazy — it's filling in every gap you left with its single most statistically average guess. Give it more of the five parts below and the gaps close, and the output stops being average.

The Five Parts
1 — Task
What you actually want done, stated as a clear instruction, not a vague topic.
"Write about productivity" is a topic. "Write a 200-word Instagram caption about why multitasking hurts productivity" is a task.
2 — Context
The background the model needs that it can't otherwise guess — who it's for, what's already been tried, what matters here.
Without context the model defaults to the most generic, average version of an answer. Context narrows it toward your specific situation.
3 — Format
How you want the output structured — a list, a table, a script with timestamps, a specific word count.
Without a format instruction you get whatever shape the model defaults to, which is rarely the shape you actually need to use.
4 — Constraints
Tone, length, things to explicitly avoid. "Casual, not corporate" or "no emojis, no exclamation marks."
Constraints remove options just as effectively as instructions add them — telling it what NOT to do is just as useful as telling it what to do.
5 — Examples
A sample of the style or structure you want, even just one or two lines.
Showing beats describing. "Write it like this: [example]" works more reliably than trying to describe a style in the abstract.
Anatomy of a real prompt, labeled

Same prompt, all five parts present. Notice how little of it is the actual "task" — most of what makes a prompt strong is everything around it.

You Don't Need All Five Every Time

A quick throwaway question doesn't need a full five-part prompt. This framework is for the prompts that actually matter — the ones you'll reuse, the ones going into real work, the ones where "good enough" output isn't good enough. Match the effort to the stakes.

Chapter 02 — Show, Don't Just Tell

Context & Examples

The two parts of the anatomy that do the most work, and the two most people skip entirely.

Of the five parts from Chapter 1, context and examples are where the real quality jump happens. Task, format and constraints tell the model what to do — context and examples teach it how to think about your specific situation.

Good Context vs Padding

Context isn't "write a lot of background" — it's the specific facts the model couldn't otherwise guess. Padding is generic scene-setting that doesn't change the output at all.

Padding (skip this)Real Context (include this)
"I run a business and want to grow it""I sell £19 fitness ebooks to an Instagram audience of 4K, mostly men 18-25"
"I'm not very good at writing""My usual tone is casual and direct, similar to how I'd text a friend"
"This is important to me""This goes out to 2,000 email subscribers tomorrow morning"
Few-Shot Prompting: The Single Best Upgrade

"Few-shot" just means giving one or two examples instead of zero. It's the single highest-leverage change you can make to almost any prompt. Instead of describing a tone in the abstract ("make it punchy"), paste one sentence that already has the tone you want and say "match this style." Models are far better at matching a pattern they can see than guessing at an adjective.

Zero-Shot vs Few-Shot

Zero-shot: "Write a punchy hook for a fitness video." Few-shot: "Write a hook in this style: 'Most people will never know this about gaining muscle.' Same energy, new topic: morning routines." The second version gives the model something concrete to pattern-match against, instead of an abstract adjective to interpret.

Chapter 03 — Ready to Use

Content Creation Prompts

Templates for TikTok, YouTube and email — fill in the brackets and go.

These templates already have the five anatomy parts built in. Swap the brackets for your specifics and you'll get a far stronger first draft than typing a bare request from scratch.

TikTok Hook Generator
"Give me 5 hook lines for a TikTok about [topic], for an audience of [audience]. Match this energy: '[paste an example hook you like]'. Under 12 words each."
YouTube Script Outline
"Outline a [length]-minute YouTube video about [topic] for [audience]. Structure: hook, problem, 3 main points, summary, CTA. Tone: [your tone]."
Email Subject Lines
"Write 8 subject lines for an email about [topic/offer]. Mix curiosity and direct-benefit styles. No clickbait that the email doesn't deliver on. Under 50 characters."
Caption + Hashtag Pack
"Write a caption for [platform] about [post topic], [length] max, [tone]. Then add 8 relevant hashtags mixing broad and niche."
Always Treat Output as a Draft

Even a perfectly-prompted output is a strong first draft, not a finished post. The model doesn't know your exact voice the way you do — read everything before posting, and trim anything that doesn't sound like you'd actually say it.

Chapter 04 — Beyond Content

Business & Productivity Prompts

AI isn't just for captions. These prompts handle the unglamorous admin that eats your week.

Content prompts get all the attention, but the bigger time savings often live in the boring tasks — emails, summaries, structuring messy notes into something usable. These are genuinely high-leverage uses most people never try.

Email Draft
"Draft a reply to this email: [paste email]. I want to say [your key points] but keep it professional and under 100 words."
Meeting Notes → Action Items
"Here are my messy meeting notes: [paste notes]. Turn this into a clean list of action items with who's responsible for each."
Decision Helper
"I'm deciding between [option A] and [option B] for [context]. List the real trade-offs of each — don't just tell me which one to pick."
Document Summariser
"Summarise this in 5 bullet points, focused specifically on [what you actually need from it]: [paste document]."
Ask It to Push Back

A genuinely underused trick: ask the model to challenge your plan instead of just executing it. "Here's my plan: [plan]. What's the strongest argument against this?" surfaces blind spots a pure "do this for me" prompt never will, because agreement is the model's default mode unless you specifically ask for the opposite.

Chapter 05 — Breaking It Down

Prompt Chaining

One giant prompt for a complex task usually fails. A chain of smaller ones usually works.

Asking for a complete, polished, ready-to-publish product in one shot for anything genuinely complex usually produces something shallow across the board rather than strong anywhere. Breaking the task into stages, reviewing each before moving on, consistently produces better results than one giant ask.

A Real Chain Example

Say you want a full email newsletter. Instead of "write me a newsletter about X," chain it:

1
Brainstorm
"Give me 5 angles for a newsletter about [topic]." Pick the best one.
↓
2
Outline
"Outline that angle as 4 sections." Check the structure works before writing a word of final copy.
↓
3
Draft
"Write section 1 in full." One section at a time, not the whole thing at once.
↓
4
Tighten
"Cut this by a third without losing the key point." A separate edit pass catches what a single draft pass misses.
Why This Actually Works Better

Each step has a narrow, achievable job instead of one prompt trying to handle ideation, structure, writing and editing simultaneously. You also get a checkpoint after every stage to redirect before errors compound into the final draft — catching a bad outline before writing is far cheaper than rewriting a bad full draft.

Chapter 06 — Level Up

Advanced Techniques

Three techniques that separate people who get good output from people who get great output.

Everything so far works for the vast majority of everyday prompts. These three techniques are for when you need the model to actually reason carefully, not just pattern-match to a quick answer.

Role Framing
"Act as a skeptical editor reviewing this for a major publication" produces sharper, more critical feedback than just "review this."
A role narrows the model toward a specific lens and standard, rather than a generic helpful-assistant default.
Step-by-Step Reasoning
"Think through this step by step before giving your final answer" measurably improves accuracy on anything involving logic, maths, or multi-part decisions.
Forcing visible intermediate steps catches errors that jumping straight to a conclusion would silently produce.
Self-Critique Pass
"Now review your own answer above. What's the weakest part of it?" — then ask it to revise based on its own critique.
A model reviewing its own prior output with fresh framing often catches issues it didn't flag the first time round.
These Are Tools, Not Magic Words

None of these techniques guarantee a perfect answer — they shift the odds. Use them for the prompts that actually matter (the ones in Chapter 1's "match effort to stakes" rule), not as a ritual on every single message. Overusing technique on trivial requests just slows you down for no real benefit.

Chapter 07 — Learn From Others

Common Mistakes

The same four habits explain most disappointing AI output.

"AI isn't very good" is usually "the prompt didn't give it enough to work with" in disguise. These four habits cause most of the frustration people blame on the tool itself.

The Four Habits to Break
The HabitWhat Goes Wrong
One-shot expectationsExpecting a perfect result first try on a complex task — refine instead of restarting
No context givenThe model fills gaps with generic averages, producing generic output
Vague feedback when refining"Make it better" gives the model nothing concrete to change — say specifically what's wrong
Accepting the first draftTreating output as finished instead of as a strong starting point to edit
"Make It Better" vs Specific Feedback

"Make it better" gives the model nothing to actually act on differently. "This is too formal — make it sound like I'm texting a friend, and cut the second paragraph entirely" gives it concrete, actionable direction. Specific feedback when refining matters as much as a good initial prompt.

A Disappointing First Output Is Information

If the result misses the mark, that's a signal about what was missing from the prompt — usually context, a format instruction, or an example — not proof the tool can't help with this. Add the missing piece and try again rather than concluding it doesn't work for this kind of task.
