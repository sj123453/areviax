Hour 1–12: Mine What You Already Know

Open your DMs, comments, and any messages from the last 3 months. Search for the same question being asked more than once. That repeated question is your idea — you don't need to invent one, you need to notice the one that's already been asked of you for free.

Where to LookWhat You're Hunting For
Your own comments/DMsA question asked more than once, in different words
Competitor reviews (1-3 star)What people wished the product also did
Reddit / niche Facebook groupsPosts that get "same!!" replies — shared frustration
Hour 12–36: Narrow It Brutally

Take whatever you found and cut it down until it's almost embarrassingly specific. "Help people get fit" is not an idea — it's a category. "A 4-week plan for skinny guys who can't gain weight no matter what they eat" is an idea. Specific sells; vague doesn't, because vague convinces no one that you understand their exact situation.

The Embarrassingly Specific Test

If your idea could describe ten different products, it's too broad. If a stranger reading the description would say "wait, that's exactly me" — you've narrowed it enough. Narrow ideas convert better than broad ones because the person reading it feels personally seen, not generically advertised to.

Hour 36–48: Sanity Check, Then Commit

Post about the problem (not the solution yet) and watch the reaction. Message 3-5 people directly and ask if they've struggled with this. If at least one says "I'd pay for that right now" unprompted — you have enough to move. Waiting for total certainty here just delays the only thing that actually proves an idea: a real launch.

Chapter 03 — Remove the Risk

Price to Convert

For a first sale specifically, the question isn't "what's it worth" — it's "what removes the last reason to say no."

A stranger with zero reviews and no track record is asking for trust they haven't earned yet. The price has to do some of that trust-building work for them. This chapter isn't full pricing theory — it's the specific tactical call for a first product from an unknown seller.

The Low-Risk Entry Price

For a genuinely first product from someone with no track record, price low enough that saying yes barely registers as a decision — typically under £20-30. This isn't permanent; it's a deliberate trust-building entry point. Once you have reviews, results, and a track record, your next product can charge what it's actually worth. The first one's job is different: prove the system works and create a customer, not maximise revenue per sale.

Free Is Not a Strategy

Giving the product away for free feels safer, but it attracts the wrong evidence. Free downloads measure curiosity, not commitment — most free downloads never get opened. A £9-19 product measures something free never can: whether a stranger will actually pull out a card. That's the real first-sale evidence you need, and free can't give it to you.

The Two-Price Test

If you genuinely can't decide between two prices, don't agonise — run both. Offer it at price A for the first 48 hours of your launch window, then price B for the next 48. Whichever converts more sales (not just clicks) is your answer for next time. This removes guesswork from a decision people usually just argue with themselves about for days.

Chapter 04 — Strangers, Not Fans

Cold-Traffic Sales Pages

A page written for people who already trust you fails completely on someone seeing your name for the first time.

If your only traffic source right now is your own small following, most of this still applies — but the real test is whether the page works on someone who has never seen you before. Cold traffic has zero accumulated trust, no context, and will leave in seconds if the page assumes otherwise.

What Changes for a Stranger
Warm Audience PageCold Traffic Page Needs
Can reference "as you know..."Must explain everything from zero, no assumed context
Trust is already built inNeeds proof elements immediately — credentials, results, specifics
Personality alone can carry itNeeds the offer itself to do more of the convincing
Casual tone works fineEvery claim needs to survive a skeptical first read
The First Viewport Test

Whatever's visible before any scrolling has to answer three questions instantly: what is this, who is it for, and why should I believe you. If a cold visitor can't answer all three within about 5 seconds, they're gone — and unlike a warm follower, they have no existing reason to stick around and figure it out.

Borrowed Credibility

You don't need your own track record yet if you can borrow someone else's. Cite a study, a well-known expert's idea, or a recognisable framework that supports your approach. "Based on the same principle [known expert] uses for X" lends credibility a brand-new seller doesn't have on their own — used honestly, this isn't deceptive, it's just accurate context.

Kill the Obvious Objections

A stranger's unspoken objections are predictable: "is this a scam," "will this actually work for someone like me," "what if I don't like it." Address these directly rather than hoping they don't think of them — a visible refund policy, a specific (not vague) description of who it's for, and at least one piece of real proof all reduce the friction a cold visitor feels before paying.

Chapter 06 — The Final Push

Getting Your First Sale

Interest doesn't pay your bills. This chapter is about converting interest into an actual payment.

Plenty of people get likes, comments and "this looks great!" without a single sale. That gap between interest and payment is where most first attempts stall. Closing it isn't about being pushier — it's about removing the specific small frictions that let an interested person quietly never get around to it.

Why "I'll Buy It Later" Usually Means Never

"Later" isn't a real plan in someone else's head — it's a polite way of not deciding now. The fix isn't pressure, it's making "now" easy and obvious: a working link in the first comment, a direct message instead of hoping they find it, a clear deadline that gives "later" an actual expiry.

Personal Outreach Beats Public Posting

A public post reaches everyone equally — interested and indifferent alike. A direct message to the 5-10 people who showed real signal earlier (commented, asked questions, reacted to your story) reaches exactly the people most likely to actually buy. Personal outreach feels like more effort because it is — and it converts far better because of it.

Chapter 07 — Diagnose, Don't Guess

Why First Sales Stall

A launch can run perfectly and still produce zero sales. Here's how to tell exactly why.

"Nobody bought" isn't one problem — it's usually one of several specific problems, each with a completely different fix. Diagnosing which one happened matters more than just trying harder next time with the same approach.

The Failure Points — And What To Actually Do About Each One
Nobody clicked the link at all
Traffic problem — wrong audience, or the post never got real reach.
Do this: Check impressions, not just clicks. Low impressions means the post itself didn't get pushed — the hook needs work (Ch.3), not the offer. Decent impressions but near-zero clicks means the caption never made anyone want to leave the app — rewrite the call-to-action to be specific about what's waiting on the other side.
People clicked, then bounced within seconds
Headline-match problem — what they clicked on and what they landed on don't feel like the same promise.
Do this: Put your post copy and your page headline side by side. If they don't use close to the same words for the same promise, that gap is the bounce. Rewrite the headline to mirror the exact thing the post promised — not a rephrased or "improved" version of it.
People read the whole page, still didn't buy
Trust/proof problem — the cold-traffic trust test from Ch.4 wasn't actually passed.
Do this: Find the first place on the page you make a claim with no specific number, screenshot, or named result backing it up — that's almost always where a full reader quietly decides not to buy. Add one concrete piece of proof there before touching anything else on the page.
People said "interested" but didn't follow through
Urgency problem — "later" had no real deadline attached to it.
Do this: Message them directly with an actual date, not a vague nudge — "cart closes Friday, want me to hold your spot?" A specific real deadline converts interest into a decision; a general reminder just gets scrolled past a second time.
People asked questions, then went quiet
Price or fit problem — the offer didn't clearly solve their specific version of the problem.
Do this: Re-read your own answer to their question — did it actually address what they asked, or just restate the sales page? If it was a real answer and they still went quiet, ask directly: "is it the price, or does this not quite fit what you need?" Most people won't volunteer that on their own, but will answer if asked.
People bought, then asked for a refund or seemed confused
Delivery-mismatch problem — what arrived didn't match what the page promised, or nobody told them what happens next.
Do this: Compare your sales page's exact wording against what a buyer actually receives — line by line, not from memory. Then add a short "here's what happens next" message immediately after purchase; most post-purchase confusion is really just silence at the one moment someone most wants reassurance.
The funnel — where each failure point actually sits

Knowing which level of the funnel people are dropping out at narrows down which failure point applies — and rules the others out.

Check Your Actual Numbers Before Diagnosing

If you have any analytics at all (link clicks, page views), check them before guessing. Zero clicks and 50 clicks-zero-sales are completely different problems requiring opposite fixes — one needs more/better traffic, the other needs a better page or offer. Diagnosing blind wastes the next attempt on the wrong fix.

One Quiet Launch Is Data, Not a Verdict

Most products that eventually sell well had an unremarkable first attempt. Find which failure point applied, fix that one thing specifically, and run the sequence again — a second attempt with one specific fix outperforms a first attempt repeated identically.
